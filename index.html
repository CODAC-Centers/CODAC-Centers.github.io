CODAC

AFOSR Center of Excellence

Compositional decision-making for modern systems

Optimization, dynamics, and control are analyzed on separate mathematical foundations. We are building one foundation that spans them, so that the properties engineers prove about parts survive in the systems they are built into.

The research plan Who we are

Modern autonomous systems are complex enough that designing and operating them takes several disciplines of engineering at once. To reach a destination, a swarm of drones may formulate an optimization problem encoding its dynamical constraints, solve that problem to plan a trajectory, and then regulate to that trajectory with an onboard controller. Those three considerations are usually analyzed individually, on disparate mathematical foundations.

That separation hides problems. Each subsystem can be optimal in isolation while their composite is not, in ways that are hard to detect. Stable subsystems can compose into an unstable whole. Resources can sit persistently misallocated because no single analysis sees the whole allocation.

DIGICAMS stands for Designing Integrated, Generic, and Interoperable Compositional Analyses of Modern Systems. It develops unified mathematical frameworks for the composition of decision-making processes across optimization, dynamical systems, and control. “Composition” here means modeling interconnections of systems along with the properties and behaviors those interconnections produce. The center was awarded by AFOSR on 19 May 2025.

What makes this different

Prior work has used category theory to model engineering problems and left the solutions in the language of category theory. We start from engineering problems, use category theory to model, generalize, and solve them, and then translate the solutions back into the engineering formalisms they came from, so that working knowledge of category theory is not a prerequisite for benefiting from the result.

Research thrusts

The work is organized into five thrusts, each with its own lead.

Computational Sheaf Theory

Thrust 1: abstractions and algorithms in computational sheaf theory, general enough to span optimization, dynamics, and control, concrete enough to implement.

Learning and Control for Open Systems

Thrust 2: composite energy-driven systems, spanning learning systems that optimize objectives and dynamical systems that obey classical mechanics.

Compositional Optimization Algorithms

Thrust 3: cellular sheaves and homological programs for decentralized optimization under impaired, asynchronous communication.

Context-Aware Decisions Under Uncertainty

Thrust 4: Moore machine formulations of POMDPs and stochastic games, and policies robust to several kinds of uncertainty at once.

Scalable Implementation and Deployment

Thrust 5: a user-facing platform and domain-specific languages that put the center’s methods in the hands of practicing engineers.
