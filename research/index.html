Components of compositional decision-making

Consider a vehicle stranded in an unknown environment, and an attempt to deploy a flock of unmanned drones to make contact with it and observe its surroundings. The flock is heterogeneous: drones differ in communication and computation capability, so they generate and share information asynchronously. Weather is uncertain until they arrive.

Decisions must be made quickly about

  1. the number of drones to deploy, a discrete optimization problem;
  2. the best routes to fly, a real-valued optimization problem that depends on the number of drones and their heterogeneous capabilities;
  3. the communication pattern of the flock, which depends on both the number of agents and the routes selected; and
  4. adaptations to unknown or unmodeled effects in the environment, such as weather or interlopers.

This one scenario requires discrete and continuous optimization, high-level planning and low-level control, robustness to both uncertain conditions and uncertain information flows, and real-time adaptation of control strategies. Decisions at each level of abstraction affect the scope of possible decisions at the others. Solving it requires not only addressing each form of decision-making but the relationships between them, such as how dispatching more drones affects communication delays and hence collective stability. That calls for a holistic, unified mathematical approach, which is what the center uses category theory to build.

Mathematical framework

The approach uses categories of systems: problems are the objects, and the structural relationships between problems are the morphisms. Categorical semantics are assigned to systems by functors that preserve this structure. This leads to representing problems and solutions as two kinds of sheaves:

  • the continuous kind, sheaves over a topological space, used for theory;
  • the combinatorial kind, sheaves over the subobjects of a combinatorial object, used for computation. Cellular sheaves are the well-studied special case.

These sheaves take semantics in each of the following categories, ordered from most structured to least.

CategoryUsed for
$\mathbf{Vect}$Numerical computing. Problems from continuous mathematics, such as optimization, dynamics and control, become systems of linear equations solved with high-performance linear algebra, with the standard tools of geometry, topology, analysis, and algebra.
$\mathbf{Met}$Contractive processes, such as asymptotically stable dynamics and convergent optimization, where fixed-point iteration serves for analysis or computation.
$\mathbf{Sup}$Optimal decision problems where an optimal satisfying solution is sought over a discrete search space, as in combinatorial or nondeterministic settings.
$\mathbf{Set}$Decision problems sending a problem to its set of solutions: finding one or enumerating all feasible solutions, solved with dynamic programming.

Novelty of the approach

The approach is unusual in using category theory to drive practical improvements in engineering applications. Prior work has used category theory to model existing problems and solve them mathematically, leaving the solutions in the language of category theory. The center’s work begins with engineering problems, uses category theory to model, generalize, and solve them, and then translates those solutions back into the original engineering formalisms they came from.

Making the categorical formalisms understandable to other engineering researchers is a central focus of the effort in its own right. Because the solutions are translated back into standard engineering formalisms, working knowledge of category theory is not a prerequisite to benefiting from the work.

Expected outcomes

Through compositional decision procedures, the center aims to expand the performance envelopes of autonomous decision systems while reducing resource burdens. Existing decision procedures constrain the decision spaces they act over, often implicitly. Partition a team of autonomous agents into two subteams, one photographing an area and the other enforcing a keepout zone, and each subteam’s task can be optimized separately. The resulting collective behavior need not be optimal. Compositional procedures divide up the act of decision-making while preserving the full mathematical structure of the decision space, with the aim of decisions that use fewer resources and perform better than current methods.

We also expect the work to make compositional decision-making tractable for other researchers, and category theory a working tool in control and optimization rather than a specialist interest.

The center also develops software that transitions its outcomes to the DoD.
