Computational Sheaf Theory

Leads: Fairbanks, Spivak, and Riess

Compositional computing for optimization, dynamics, and control must be general enough to encapsulate these different disciplines, including their different representations of uncertainty, yet concrete enough to provide implementable algorithms. New methods must also account for where computations are executed and how those computations are interdependent among decision makers.

This thrust develops new abstractions and algorithms using computational sheaf theory in order to abstract and unify the disciplines the center considers. Sheaf theory cuts across all of the research thrusts, and the developments here help operationalize the research in the other four.

Sheaves come in two kinds. Continuous sheaves, defined over a topological space, carry the theory. Combinatorial sheaves, defined over the subobjects of a combinatorial object, carry the computation, and cellular sheaves are their well-studied special case.
